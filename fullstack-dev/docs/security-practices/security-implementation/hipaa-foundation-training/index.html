HIPAA Foundation Training & Assessment (Healthcare Projects)

Overview​

This document defines the baseline HIPAA foundation training requirement for development teams working on healthcare projects (or any project that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits Protected Health Information (PHI) on behalf of a covered entity or business associate). The goal is to ensure engineers understand HIPAA fundamentals and can demonstrate competence by passing an assessment after training.

Policy Statement​

  • Team members who build, review, deploy, or operate systems that handle PHI must complete HIPAA foundation training.
  • Team members must pass the post-training assessment within the required time window.
  • Training must be completed during onboarding and refreshed on a defined cadence.

Scope​

Applies to:

  • Backend engineers (NestJS), frontend engineers (Next.js), SRE/DevOps, QA, and security engineers
  • Contractors and vendors with access to in-scope environments, logs, or support tooling

In-scope systems typically include:

  • application services that process PHI
  • databases/object stores containing PHI
  • logging/monitoring systems that could capture PHI
  • CI/CD, identity, network controls, and admin tooling that can impact PHI confidentiality/integrity/availability

Training Requirements​

1) Training content (minimum)​

HIPAA foundation training must cover:

  • HIPAA goals and why it matters for healthcare systems
  • Key terms and definitions (high level):
    • PHI (Protected Health Information)
    • covered entity vs. business associate (high level)
    • the “minimum necessary” principle
  • The three HIPAA safeguard categories (high level):
    • administrative safeguards
    • physical safeguards
    • technical safeguards
  • Common engineering risks:
    • PHI exposure through logs, error messages, analytics, and URLs
    • overly broad access to production systems
    • insecure backups, exports, and support tooling
  • Practical engineering behaviors:
    • least privilege, strong authentication (including MFA), and access reviews
    • encryption in transit and at rest; key/secrets management
    • safe logging (redaction) and incident escalation
    • secure data lifecycle (collection, retention, deletion)

1a) Comprehensive training curriculum (recommended)​

This curriculum can be used to build an internal LMS course (60–120 minutes total) plus a short practical exercise.

Module A — HIPAA fundamentals for engineers​

Learning objectives:

  • Explain what PHI is and identify it in typical healthcare product flows.
  • Understand that PHI can appear in unexpected places (logs, attachments, free-text).

Key topics:

  • PHI vs. non-PHI data; direct and indirect identifiers (engineering perspective)
  • Data lifecycle: collection → storage → access → sharing → retention → deletion

Hands-on exercise:

  • Given a sample “patient + appointment + billing” schema, mark which fields are PHI and identify which are high-risk (e.g., diagnoses, lab results, images, notes).

Module B — Minimum necessary and data minimization​

Learning objectives:

  • Apply “minimum necessary” thinking to system design and day-to-day changes.
  • Reduce PHI footprint across services, logs, and third parties.

Key topics:

  • Minimizing data collection and limiting data propagation
  • Avoiding PHI in analytics and client-side instrumentation
  • Safe identifiers: when to use pseudonymous IDs instead of PHI

Hands-on exercise:

  • Review a proposed new event/log payload and remove any PHI fields; propose safe alternatives.

Module C — Access control and privileged operations​

Learning objectives:

  • Implement least privilege for PHI systems and admin tooling.
  • Understand operational controls for production access.

Key topics:

  • Role-based access control, approvals, and break-glass workflows
  • Access reviews and separation of duties (high level)
  • Auditing privileged access (what should be logged and why)

Hands-on exercise:

  • Propose a minimal set of roles for “support access to PHI” and what actions must be audited.

Module D — Technical safeguards in the NestJS/Next.js + GCP stack​

Learning objectives:

  • Place technical controls where they naturally belong in the stack.
  • Avoid common leak paths and unsafe patterns.

Key topics:

  • NestJS: DTO validation, authz guards, secure serialization, audit events
  • Next.js: safe caching, avoiding PHI in URLs, safe error pages
  • GCP/Kubernetes: secrets handling, service accounts, restricted logs, storage encryption
  • Secure logging: redact PHI by default; avoid request-body logging in production

Hands-on exercise (choose one):

  • Review an API handler and remove unsafe logging; replace with structured, redacted logs.
  • Identify where PHI might be cached (browser, CDN, server cache) and apply a safe caching strategy.

Module E — Incident response basics for PHI exposure​

Learning objectives:

  • Recognize PHI incidents and escalate quickly.
  • Preserve evidence while containing exposure.

Key topics:

  • What constitutes a PHI exposure (examples)
  • Containment vs. eradication vs. recovery (engineering operational lens)
  • Evidence preservation and secure handling

Hands-on exercise:

  • Draft an “incident trigger” checklist for suspected PHI leakage (logs, misconfigured bucket, unauthorized access).

2) Assessment (pass/fail)​

  • Engineers must pass a knowledge check after training.
  • Recommended passing score: ≥ 80%.
  • Failed attempts must be remediated with a re-take after reviewing incorrect topics.

Assessment blueprint (recommended)​

  • Format: 20–30 questions (multiple-choice + scenario-based)
  • Time limit: 20–30 minutes
  • Passing score: ≥ 80%
  • Coverage (example weighting):
    • 30%: PHI identification + minimum necessary
    • 30%: access control + auditing expectations
    • 25%: technical safeguards (logging, encryption, secrets)
    • 15%: incident recognition + escalation

Scenario examples:

  • A developer proposes logging full request bodies for a “patient intake” endpoint. What risks does this create and how do you design safe logging?
  • A frontend change proposes putting patientName in a URL query parameter for convenience. What’s wrong with this and what’s a safer alternative?
  • An alert indicates unusual access to a database containing PHI. What is the immediate containment action and who must be notified?

Operational Process​

Onboarding​

  • New joiners complete training within 30 days of start date (or before production access to PHI systems is granted, whichever is earlier).
  • Access to PHI production systems requires either:
    • confirmed completion + passing assessment, or
    • an approved, time-bound exception with compensating controls.

Refresher cadence​

  • Refresh training annually, and additionally when:
    • PHI data flows change (new features, new integrations, new vendors)
    • major incidents occur (or near-miss events)
    • internal standards change (logging policy, access policy, SDLC gates)

Ownership​

  • Security/Compliance owns the training curriculum and assessment maintenance.
  • Engineering managers ensure completion for their teams.
  • Platform/SRE enforces access gating where feasible.

Evidence & Auditability​

Maintain evidence for audits and internal governance:

  • Training completion records (user, course, date)
  • Assessment result (score, pass/fail, date)
  • Exceptions and expiry dates (if any)

Recommended storage locations:

  • LMS export or compliance tracker (GRC tool)
  • Ticket references for exceptions

Website Resources (Recommended)​

  • U.S. Department of Health & Human Services (HHS) HIPAA: https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/
  • HIPAA Privacy Rule (HHS): https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/
  • HIPAA Security Rule (HHS): https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/security/
  • OWASP Cheat Sheet Series (secure practices supporting HIPAA outcomes): https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/
  • Google Cloud Security documentation (for platform controls): https://cloud.google.com/security
  • Kubernetes Security documentation: https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/security/

Acceptance Criteria (Definition of Done)​

  • 100% of in-scope team members have completed HIPAA foundation training.
  • 100% have passed the assessment (or have an approved, time-bound exception).
  • Training completion and scores are retrievable for audits.
  • Access to PHI production systems is gated by completion status where feasible.
